## 1. Title

Incrementalist research is fine, actually. Seth Ariel Green, Humane & Sustainable Food Lab, Stanford University. Plant Food System Summit, Berlin, November 2026.

## 2. A question worth asking

Richard Hamming, in "You and Your Research" (1986), used to ask scientists at Bell Labs: "What are the important problems of your field?" And later: "If what you are doing is not important, and if you don't think it is going to lead to something important, why are you ... working on it?" Tyler Cowen asks a version of this too: "What are the most important unsolved problems in your field?"

## 3. The most important question in our field

I think the most important question in our field is: what will meaningfully reduce consumption of meat and animal products? What will be most effective at doing this? What will be most cost-effective? Whom should we target with what?

## 4. I wrote one paper that addresses this directly

I wrote one paper that addresses this directly: our meta-analysis of interventions intended to reduce consumption of meat and animal products, "Meaningfully reducing consumption of meat and animal products is an unsolved problem." Basically nothing works.

## 5. But most of my papers are not about this

But most of my papers are not about this at all. Most of my papers are about much smaller, but perhaps more foundational, issues: measurement error, small changes, and how big an effect we should expect to see from those changes. For example: TACOS, where adding plant-based options to a Chipotle-like menu cut meat by about one percentage point per option; and PALATE, where adding alt proteins to seven real restaurants did not reduce meat purchases.

## 7. Why do I do this?

Right now our field is still not a mature field, not the way economics is. We have many really basic questions that are not answered at the methodological level. So if someone tests their theory the way a lot of papers currently test such things, we won't actually learn what meaningfully reduces consumption from those studies, because the design and measurement strategies are not yet there, in general, to make reasonable inference possible.

This is a big problem, and it's one we can crack from many angles. I think it's the problem many of us should be cracking at, even if it looks like we're not working on the most important problem I named earlier.

## 8. Fixing the whole field is really hard

Fixing these problems at the level of the field, rather than at the level of any individual paper, is going to be really hard. So thank goodness Kristof Dhont et al. are working on that, through PHAIR! But personally, I think it's fine to either evaluate the incrementalist stuff that actually gets deployed in the real world, or do more meta-level work on measurement error and design. Tastes may vary.

And in general, I'm curious to hear from other people: if you're not working on the big question of what reduces meat consumption effectively, why not? What's your theory of change?
